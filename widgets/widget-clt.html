How to play

The paper plane club scores every plane by its average distance over several throws, so one lucky throw can’t win the day.

Each round names a plane and how many throws make one score. The middle chart shows where a single throw of that plane lands.

Drag the zone on the bottom chart to where you think 9 scores in 10 will land. Drag its edges to resize it, or its middle to move it.

Fly 1,000 rounds. You’re on target if the zone catches at least 900 scores and is not much wider than it needs to be.

Averages settle into a bell

One throw of the Looper lands near 3 m or near 10 m, and almost never in between. Its score, the average of many throws, lands in between: near its true average of 6.5 m, in a bell around it. The same happens for every plane, whatever single throws look like. The bell also narrows as throws are added, but slowly. The spread shrinks with the square root of the number of throws, so four times as many throws only halves it.

This is the central limit theorem. The throws are i.i.d. draws X₁, …, Xₙ with mean μ and standard deviation σ, and the score is the sample mean. For large n the sample mean is approximately normal with mean μ and standard error σ/√n, whatever the distribution of a single throw, so about 90% of sample means land within μ ± 1.645 σ/√n. The Tumbler is a uniform distribution, the Glider an exponential distribution shifted by 1.5 m, and the Looper a bimodal mixture of two normals.

Each round is one batch of 1,000 simulated scores, with no redrawing to favor an outcome. A zone that covers 90% of the population can catch fewer than 900 in one batch. The verdict describes the batch on screen: a zone up to a quarter wider than its narrowest 900-score zone, or 0.2 m wider for very narrow zones, is still on target.
