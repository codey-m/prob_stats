How to play

Lost coins hide under the sand, more where people gather: hardly any in the quiet dunes, plenty under the pier.

Your detector beeps over most coins. Bottle caps set it off too, and damp or wet sand makes it beep more often.

At each spot the detector sweeps 100 squares. Then you choose: dig up every beep, or walk on to the next spot.

Follow the odds. Digging has a positive expected return when more than 3 beeps in 10 are coins on average. A good call can still lose points on one beach. Each dig costs 3 points and each coin is worth 10.

A day is five spots. Make the call that pays at every one.

A beep means more where coins are common

The detector is just as good at every spot on the same ground, but a beep means different things in different places. In the dunes only 2 squares in 100 hide a coin, so the 98 empty squares make most of the beeps, even if each one sets it off only now and then. Under the pier, 30 squares hide a coin, and real beeps outnumber false ones. How common coins are to begin with, the base rate, decides what a beep is worth.

In this lecture's terms a coin is the condition D and a beep is a positive test. The place sets the base rate P(D). The ground sets the sensitivity P(+ | D) and the false-positive rate P(+ | not D), which is 1 minus the specificity. The share of beeps that are coins is P(D | +), from Bayes' rule: P(D | +) = P(+ | D)P(D) / [P(+ | D)P(D) + P(+ | not D)P(not D)]. Digging has a positive expected return when P(D | +) is above 3/10, the cost of a dig over the value of a coin.

The calculation is Bayes' rule, from Probability & Statistical Data Analysis (6.3710.1x) Lec. 2, Conditioning and Bayes' rule.

Each beach is one random sweep with independent coin locations and detector outcomes. No outcomes are discarded. The verdict judges your choice using the rates; the points report what happened on this beach. The percentage comes from Bayes' rule, not from the coins found on a particular sweep.
