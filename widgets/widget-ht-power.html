    How to play

    The claim is that a friend’s new fold flies 1.5 m farther than a standard paper plane. The goal is the quickest test that backs a real gain at least 80% of the time, and backs a fold that does nothing only 5% of the time.

    Throw both folds. You throw every plane yourself, a standard plane and then a wonder fold, so both get the same arm and the same moment. Every throw still lands somewhere different: throws wobble, wind gusts, and measuring is rough.

    Compare the averages. The test compares how far each fold flew on average. The more flights, the less the averages wobble.

    Back the claim only on strong evidence. The wonder fold’s average must land past a line, placed so that a fold that does nothing gets past it only 5 times in 100.

    Significance level α sets how often a fold that does nothing gets backed anyway.

    Wind and measuring choices remove noise but take time. Flight pairs take 2 minutes each.

    Run the test to watch one test play out. Switch to does nothing to see how often luck fools the test.

    Removing noise buys evidence faster than adding flights

    The chance a test catches a real effect is called its power. For this one-sided test it depends on the gain divided by how much the average difference varies, which is the spread of one comparison divided by the square root of the number of flight pairs. The false-alarm rate stays at 5 in 100 the whole time: a better plan raises the power without making the test easier to fool.

    The two bells are this lecture’s picture of a one-sided z-test. H₀ says the fold does nothing and H₁ says it adds the gain. Backing the claim when H₀ is true is a Type I error, and its probability is the significance level α, the red area past the line. Holding back when H₁ is true is a Type II error, with probability β, the hatched part of the gold bell. Power is 1 − β. Set α to 10% and then to 1%, and watch the line, the three areas and the time the quickest reliable test needs.
